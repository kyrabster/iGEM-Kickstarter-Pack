1.1 Defining Synthetic Biology

1.1.1 A Working Definition

Key definition

Synthetic biology (synbio) is the design and construction of new biological parts, devices, and systems — and the re-design of existing biological systems for useful purposes.

Every word of this definition is doing work:

  • Design and construction — synbio approaches organisms the way an engineer approaches a bridge or a phone: as something that can be deliberately designed, not just studied.
  • New biological parts — DNA sequences (promoters, genes, switches) treated as standardised, reusable components, much like electronic components.
  • Re-design of existing systems — often the most powerful move is not building something from nothing, but re-wiring what nature already provides.

1.1.2 How Synbio Differs from What You Already Teach

You already teach molecular biology: DNA → RNA → protein, gene regulation, enzymes. Synbio uses all of that knowledge — but asks a different question.

Molecular Biology (what you teach) Synthetic Biology (what this pack introduces)
Core question How does this living system work? What could this living system do if we re-designed it?
Approach Observational, analytical Engineering-driven, constructive
Direction of inquiry Gene → function (“what does this gene do?”) Function → gene (“what gene would do this?”)
Output Understanding, publications Working systems: sensors, therapies, materials
Classroom analogue Dissecting a radio to see how it works Building a radio from a circuit diagram

Teacher analogy

Molecular biology is like studying the grammar of a language — its rules, its vocabulary, its sentence structure. Synthetic biology is like writing a poem in that language. You cannot write well without grammar, but grammar alone is not the goal. Your students already have the grammar (your syllabus); synbio gives them a reason to use it.

1.1.3 The Key Conceptual Move: Biological Parts as Standardised Components

The founding insight of synbio (formalised in the early 2000s) is that DNA sequences can be treated like standardised parts:

  • A promoter is a part that says “start reading here” (and controls how strongly).
  • An RBS (ribosome binding site) is a part that sets how much protein gets made.
  • A coding sequence is the part that encodes the actual protein.
  • A terminator is a part that says “stop reading here.”

Combine a promoter + RBS + gene + terminator on a plasmid, and you have a genetic circuit — a working “device” inside a living cell. This is why synbio borrows its vocabulary from electronics: promoters behave roughly like dials, repressors like switches, and combinations of them like logic gates.

This standardisation is also why the iGEM Registry of Standard Biological Parts (registry.igem.org) exists, and why your students will one day read plasmid maps the way electricians read circuit diagrams (Section 2.4 of Module 2 teaches exactly this).

1.2 The Engineering Cycle: How Synbio Projects Actually Work

1.2.1 Design–Build–Test–Learn

Synbio is not a collection of techniques; it is a workflow. Every iGEM project — and every industrial synbio project — follows the same loop:

1. DESIGN ──► 2. BUILD ──► 3. TEST ──► 4. LEARN
(model & plan)  (DNA assembly)  (experiment)  (analyse & revise)
        ▲                                        │
        └────────────────────────────────────────┘
  1. DESIGN — Define the problem, choose parts, and predict behaviour with a mathematical model before touching the bench (Module 2, Section 2.1).
  2. BUILD — Assemble the DNA: virtual cloning first, then physical assembly (Module 2, Section 2.4 → Module 3, Sections 3.1–3.2).
  3. TEST — Run the experiment: transform cells, measure output (fluorescence, growth, colour change) (Module 3).
  4. LEARN — Compare results to the model. Where did reality differ from prediction? Revise the design and loop again.

Teacher analogy

This is the scientific method your students already know — hypothesis, experiment, analysis, revision — but with an engineering twist: the “hypothesis” is a design specification, and the loop repeats until the system works to spec. If you teach Scientific Investigation / SBA or IB IA, the DBTL cycle is simply that process wearing an engineer’s hard hat.

1.2.2 A Worked Example: The Arsenic Biosensor

To make the cycle concrete, this pack uses one running example throughout all modules — a classic of the field and a frequent iGEM project worldwide:

Goal: Engineer E. coli that turns green (fluoresces) when arsenic is present in water.

Cycle stage What it looks like in this project Pack section
Design Choose an arsenic-responsive promoter; model expected GFP output vs. arsenic concentration; decide whether fluorescence will be detectable Module 2, Section 2.1
Build Assemble promoter + GFP gene into a plasmid (virtually, then physically) Module 2, Section 2.4 → Module 3, Section 3.2
Test Transform E. coli, expose to water samples, measure fluorescence Module 3, Section 3.2
Learn Fluorescence too weak? Model says increase promoter strength or plasmid copy number. Too leaky? Add a degradation tag. Loop again. Module 2, Section 2.1

Notice what this example demonstrates: the dry lab and the wet lab are not separate subjects — they are two halves of one loop. This is the single most important idea for your students to absorb, and it is how the modules of this pack are meant to be used together.

1.3 A Brief History of Synbio: Five Milestones You Can Teach From Your Syllabus

You do not need the full history. These five milestones are enough — and each one connects directly to a topic you already teach:

Year Milestone What Happened Curriculum Hook
1978–79 Recombinant human insulin Genentech chemically synthesised the human insulin genes and expressed them in bacteria (Goeddel et al., 1979) — approved as the first genetically engineered human drug (Humulin, 1982). Diabetic patients stopped relying on pig pancreases. HKDSE: gene technology; IB: genetic engineering; IAL: pharmaceuticals
2006–13 Yeast-made artemisinin Jay Keasling’s team engineered yeast to produce artemisinic acid, precursor of the leading anti-malaria drug — a landmark of metabolic engineering. HKDSE: microorganisms and humans; IB: metabolism
2010 First synthetic genome The J. Craig Venter Institute assembled an entire bacterial genome chemically and booted it up in a recipient cell (M. mycoides JCVI-syn1.0) — proof that a genome is, in principle, software you can write. HKDSE: molecular genetics; IB: DNA replication
2012–20 CRISPR gene editing Doudna and Charpentier’s discovery of programmable genome editing (Nobel Prize, 2020) turned genome modification from an art into a routine tool. HKDSE: biotechnology elective; IB: genetics; IAL: genes and health
2020–24 AI protein design AlphaFold 2 solved 50-year protein-structure prediction (Nobel Prize, 2024); RFdiffusion and ProteinMPNN enabled designing entirely new proteins. This is the subject of Module 2, Section 2.2. IB: proteins; IAL: protein structure

Discussion prompt for your class

“Insulin took a Nobel-adjacent industrial effort in 1978. Today, a university undergraduate team can design a biosensor in one summer. What changed?” (Answer: standardised parts, cheaper DNA synthesis, better tools — and the iGEM community. This question opens the door to explaining why now is the moment students can participate, not just read about.)

1.4 Synbio in the Real World: Applications Your Students Already Touch

Students engage when they see that synbio is not futuristic — it is already in their food, medicine, clothing, and environment. Four sectors, each with a teachable example:

Medicine - Engineered CAR-T cell therapy: a patient’s own immune cells are genetically re-programmed to hunt cancer — an approved treatment for several blood cancers. - mRNA vaccines: the COVID-19 vaccines are synthetic-biology products end to end — a designed sequence, manufactured by the patient’s own cells. - Designed protein binders: computational design of therapeutic proteins (the pipeline your students can explore in Module 2, Section 2.2).

Food & Agriculture - Precision-fermented proteins: dairy proteins (whey, casein) brewed in yeast — already on supermarket shelves in the US and Singapore. - Golden Rice: engineered to produce beta-carotene to combat vitamin A deficiency — also a superb ethics case study (Section 1.8).

Environment - Biosensors: bacteria that detect arsenic, lead, or mercury in water (our running example). - Bioremediation: engineered microbes that break down oil spills or plastic (e.g., engineered PET-degrading enzymes).

Materials & Manufacturing - Spider silk from yeast: engineered organisms producing high-performance fibres. - Bio-leather, bio-dyes, bio-cement: companies growing materials rather than extracting them.

Teacher tip

Ask students to pick one product and trace it back through the DBTL cycle: what was the design goal? What had to be built? How would it be tested? This single exercise converts a news headline into a synbio lesson.

1.5 Why This Matters for Hong Kong Students

1.5.1 The Economic Case

Hong Kong’s development strategy has placed biotechnology and innovation at the centre of its economic future — through the Innovation and Technology Commission, InnoHK research clusters, the Science Park and Cyberport ecosystems, and HKEX’s biotech listing chapter, which has made Hong Kong one of the world’s leading venues for biotech financing. Biotechnology companies in the Greater Bay Area are hiring at every level — research, regulatory, manufacturing, data science.

The bottleneck is not jobs; it is people who have touched the tools before university. That is the barrier iBRIDGE exists to remove.

1.5.2 The University Case

Students who can point to a synthetic biology project — a documented design, a model, a lab notebook, ideally an iGEM medal — stand out in admissions for:

  • HKU, CUHK, HKUST biomedical sciences, molecular biology, and biotechnology programmes
  • Overseas universities (UK: biochemistry/biotech; US: bioengineering programmes) — project-based evidence is weighted heavily
  • IB/IAL pathways: the extended project work maps directly onto IA requirements (see the curriculum tables in Modules 2 and 3)

1.5.3 The Pedagogical Case

Synbio is not an add-on to your syllabus — it is a context that makes your syllabus memorable:

  • Enzyme kinetics becomes real when students model an enzyme they might engineer (Module 2, Section 2.1).
  • Natural selection becomes real when students run directed evolution in silico (Module 2, Section 2.2).
  • Recombinant DNA becomes real when students plan a cloning strategy on screen before ever touching a pipette (Module 2, Section 2.4).

1.6 iGEM: The World’s Premier Synthetic Biology Competition

1.6.1 What iGEM Is

The International Genetically Engineered Machine (iGEM) competition began as an MIT student project course in 2003 and has grown into the world’s largest synthetic biology event, hosted annually by the non-profit iGEM Foundation. Key facts:

  • Format: Student teams (high school, undergraduate, and overgraduate tracks) spend roughly a year on a self-designed synbio project, following the full DBTL cycle, and present at the iGEM Grand Jamboree (held in Paris since 2022), which draws 400+ teams and thousands of participants from 50+ countries.
  • Deliverables: A wiki documenting the project, a laboratory notebook, a poster and presentation, and — critically — new standardised DNA parts deposited to the iGEM Registry for future teams.
  • Judging: Medals (Bronze/Silver/Gold) are criterion-based — every team that meets the criteria earns the medal; special prizes and Grand Prize awards recognise the best projects in tracks spanning diagnostics, environment, therapeutics, software, and more.
  • Values: iGEM explicitly requires safety and ethics work in every project (Section 1.8) and emphasises “human practices” — engaging with communities, stakeholders, and the public about the implications of the work.

1.6.2 What This Means for a Hong Kong School

Hong Kong has a strong and growing iGEM presence, with teams from HKU, CUHK, HKUST, and secondary schools competing regularly and earning medals and special-prize nominations. High school teams are fully supported within the competition structure, and the skills required — modelling, cloning, measurement, documentation, public engagement — map onto every module of this pack.

The honest pitch to your principal

iGEM is to synthetic biology what the Physics Olympiad is to physics — an internationally recognised, criterion-based competition that transforms an extracurricular interest into a portfolio-grade credential.

1.8 Safety, Ethics & Biosecurity: The Conversation You Will Have First

Before any teacher adopts synbio, and before any principal approves it, the same questions come up. This section gives you the honest answers — and these answers are themselves teachable content.

1.8.1 Safety: What Is Actually Being Used?

  • Organism: All protocols in this pack use E. coli K-12, a strain that has been safely used in classrooms and laboratories for over 50 years. It is classified BSL-1 (Risk Group 1) — the lowest biosafety level, the same category as the microorganisms in standard school microbiology practicals (e.g., the HKDSE Biotechnology elective’s own bacterial culture work).
  • What it cannot do: K-12 strains cannot colonise the human gut, cannot survive long outside ideal laboratory conditions, and carry no virulence factors.
  • Containment: Standard school practice — gloves, lab coats, bleach decontamination of all waste (10% bleach, 30 min), hand washing — is fully sufficient. Module 3 details every precaution.
  • Institutional oversight: Schools should follow EDB laboratory safety guidelines and their own school biosafety procedures. See also the Regulatory Note for Hong Kong Schools in Module 3, Appendix A, which explains how contained classroom work with BSL-1 organisms relates to Hong Kong’s GMO legislation. 1.8.2 Biosecurity: Could This Be Misused?

This is a fair question and deserves a direct answer. The knowledge in this pack is foundational and defensive in nature: it teaches students what standard biological parts are, how containment works, and why oversight exists. It does not enable anything dangerous — the organisms, parts, and protocols involved are the same ones used in school biology curricula worldwide. In fact, iGEM requires every team to complete a safety review and to answer “what would happen if your project were misused?” — making biosecurity literacy a formal part of the education, not an afterthought.

Teacher analogy

Teaching students to grow E. coli K-12 with a GFP plasmid is chemistry-lab safety territory, not weapons-development territory. The gap between a school biosensor and anything of concern is measured in decades of expertise, specialised facilities, and institutional oversight — none of which this pack touches.

2.1 Mathematical Modeling in Synthetic Biology

2.1.1 Why Model? (Conceptual Overview)

What this demonstrates: the DESIGN stage of the DBTL cycle — the habit of predicting before building.

Biology is messy. Living systems involve thousands of interacting components — genes, proteins, metabolites, environmental signals — all changing over time. When a student proposes a synthetic biology project (e.g., “I want to engineer E. coli to produce a fluorescent protein when it detects arsenic”), the immediate question is: will it actually work?

Mathematical modeling answers this question before you spend a single dollar on reagents. By translating biological components into equations, we can simulate how a system will behave and predict outcomes computationally.

Teacher analogy

Imagine planning a road trip. You could just start driving and hope you arrive, or you could use a map (the model) to estimate travel time, fuel costs, and the best route. Modeling in synthetic biology is the map — it doesn’t guarantee you’ll arrive, but it tells you whether the trip is feasible and helps you avoid dead ends.

The core idea: every biological process — gene expression, protein degradation, enzymatic reactions, cell growth — can be described by mathematical relationships. When we combine these relationships, we can simulate an entire genetic circuit on a computer and observe how it behaves over time, under different conditions, and with different parameters.

2.1.3 Core Mathematical Equations

You do not need calculus to teach these concepts. The equations below can be explained intuitively and simulated using free web-based tools. What follows is a non-calculus breakdown of the three most important equation families in synthetic biology modeling.

(a) Ordinary Differential Equations (ODEs) for Gene Expression

An ODE describes how a quantity changes over time. In synthetic biology, the most common model describes how mRNA and protein concentrations change:

Step 1 — mRNA (transcription):

dM/dt = (k_tx × [DNA]) − (k_m × M)

Step 2 — protein (translation):

dP/dt = (k_tl × M) − (k_deg × P)

Where:

  • M = mRNA concentration (molecules per cell)
  • P = protein concentration (molecules per cell)
  • dM/dt, dP/dt = rate of change of each concentration over time
  • k_tx = transcription rate per plasmid copy (mRNA per min per gene copy)
  • [DNA] = plasmid copy number (gene dosage)
  • k_m = mRNA degradation rate constant (per min)
  • k_tl = translation efficiency (proteins produced per mRNA per min)
  • k_deg = protein degradation rate constant (per min; includes protein turnover and dilution by cell division)

Teacher analogy (the two-bathtub picture)

There are two bathtubs in a row. The faucet of the first fills it with mRNA (transcription); its drain is mRNA degradation. A pipe carries water from the first tub to the second — that is translation — and the second tub’s drain is protein degradation. The water level in the second tub is the protein concentration. Each level settles where inflow equals outflow: that is steady state.

At steady state (both levels no longer changing, so dM/dt = 0 and dP/dt = 0):

  • From Step 1: M_steady = (k_tx × [DNA]) / k_m
  • Substituting into Step 2: P_steady = (k_tl × M_steady) / k_deg, which gives the composite steady-state equation:
P_steady = (k_tx × k_tl × [DNA]) / (k_m × k_deg)

Units walkthrough (do this once with students)

  • k_tx × [DNA]: (mRNA/min/copy) × copies = mRNA/min — an inflow rate for the mRNA tub.
  • Dividing by k_m (1/min): (mRNA/min) ÷ (1/min) = mRNA — the steady mRNA level. ✔
  • k_tl × M_steady: (protein/mRNA/min) × mRNA = protein/min — an inflow rate for the protein tub.
  • Dividing by k_deg (1/min): (protein/min) ÷ (1/min) = protein — the steady protein level. ✔

Every rate constant must appear in the denominator exactly once for the units to resolve into a concentration. This is also why a model that omits k_m is dimensionally incomplete.

This simple equation tells students that to increase protein output, they can: (1) use a stronger promoter (increase k_tx), (2) use a better RBS (increase k_tl), (3) increase plasmid copy number (increase [DNA]), (4) use a more stable protein (decrease k_deg) — or (5) use a more stable mRNA (decrease k_m). Each of these maps to a concrete experimental decision.

(b) Michaelis–Menten Kinetics for Enzymatic Reactions

Enzymes are proteins that catalyse chemical reactions. The Michaelis–Menten equation describes how fast an enzyme works at different substrate concentrations:

V = (V_max × [S]) / (K_m + [S])

Where:

  • V = reaction velocity (rate of product formation)
  • V_max = maximum reaction rate (when the enzyme is saturated with substrate)
  • [S] = substrate concentration
  • K_m = Michaelis constant — the substrate concentration at which V = V_max / 2

Teacher analogy

Imagine a cashier at a supermarket. When there are few customers (low [S]), the cashier processes them quickly but the overall throughput is low. When there are many customers (high [S]), the cashier is working at maximum speed (V_max) but can’t go any faster. K_m is the number of customers in line when the cashier is working at half their maximum speed. A low K_m means the cashier is efficient even with few customers. A high K_m means you need a lot of customers to get the cashier working hard.

Key insights for students:

  • When [S] is much less than K_m: V ≈ (V_max / K_m) × [S] — the reaction is linear in substrate concentration (first-order kinetics)
  • When [S] is much greater than K_m: V ≈ V_max — the enzyme is saturated (zero-order kinetics)
  • Lower K_m generally indicates higher apparent affinity — with one honesty note for advanced students: K_m equals the true binding dissociation constant only when the enzyme–substrate complex mostly falls apart rather than reacting; operationally, K_m is always the substrate concentration at half-maximal velocity, and that is the definition examiners want.

(c) Hill Functions for Promoter Regulation

Promoters are DNA sequences that control when and how much a gene is expressed. Many promoters are regulated by transcription factors (proteins that bind to DNA and switch genes on or off). The Hill equation describes how promoter activity changes with transcription factor concentration:

For activation (transcription factor turns the gene ON):

f([X]) = [X]^n / (K_d^n + [X]^n)

For repression (transcription factor turns the gene OFF):

f([X]) = K_d^n / (K_d^n + [X]^n)

Where:

  • [X] = transcription factor concentration
  • K_d = dissociation constant (the [X] at which the promoter is half-active)
  • n = Hill coefficient — an empirical measure of cooperativity, i.e., how steep and switch-like the response is. It is bounded above by the number of binding sites, but it is not literally the number of transcription factor molecules that bind.

Teacher analogy

Think of a light dimmer switch. The Hill function describes the relationship between how hard you press the dimmer ([X]) and how bright the light gets (promoter activity). The Hill coefficient n is like the sensitivity of the dimmer: n = 1 means a smooth, gradual response (like a regular dimmer). n > 1 means a sharp, switch-like response (like a button — it’s either off or on, with little in between). This “switch-like” behaviour is what allows cells to make decisive decisions, like committing to cell division or triggering apoptosis.

Key insights:

  • n = 1: non-cooperative binding (gradual response)
  • n > 1: cooperative binding (switch-like, ultrasensitive response)
  • n ≈ 2.8 for haemoglobin–oxygen binding — the classic textbook example: four binding sites, but the measured Hill coefficient is below 4 because binding is not perfectly concerted
  • K_d determines the threshold — the [X] at which the promoter switches

2.1.4 The Educator’s Modeling Logic Flow

This is the step-by-step workflow to teach students for modeling any synthetic biology system:

  1. PROBLEM DEFINITION — “What biological question are we trying to answer?” Example: “How much fluorescent protein will our circuit produce?”
  2. PARAMETER IDENTIFICATION — “What biological quantities affect the answer?” Example: promoter strength, RBS efficiency, degradation rates, plasmid copy number.
  3. EQUATION SETUP — “What mathematical relationship connects these quantities?” Example: the two-step ODE for gene expression (Section 2.1.3a).
  4. SIMULATION — “Let’s compute the answer using a tool.” Example: enter the equations and parameters into COPASI or a Python notebook.
  5. WET-LAB VALIDATION — “Does the prediction match reality?” Example: measure fluorescence in the lab and compare to the simulation.

Teaching tip

Steps 1–4 can be done entirely in a computer lab with no biological materials. Step 5 connects the dry lab to the wet lab (Module 3) and reinforces the scientific method: predict, test, compare, revise.

2.1.6 Classroom Exercise: Modeling a Fluorescent Protein Circuit

Objective: students predict how much fluorescent protein (GFP) a simple genetic circuit will produce, then compare their prediction to a qualitative wet-lab result (Module 3, Section 3.2.1 — the transformation practical).

Setup (computer lab, 45 minutes):

  1. Define the system (10 min): a constitutive promoter drives GFP expression on a plasmid. Students identify the parameters:
    • Promoter strength: k_tx = 10 mRNA/min/copy
    • Translation efficiency: k_tl = 5 protein/mRNA/min
    • Plasmid copy number: [DNA] = 50 copies/cell
    • mRNA degradation rate: k_m = 6 /min (typical bacterial mRNA half-life ~10 min)
    • Protein degradation rate: k_deg = 0.02 /min (GFP is relatively stable)
  2. Calculate steady state (10 min): students use the composite equation:
    • M_steady = (10 × 50) / 6 ≈ 83 mRNA per cell
    • P_steady = (10 × 5 × 50) / (6 × 0.02) = 2,500 / 0.12 ≈ 20,833 proteins per cell
    • Sanity check with the units walkthrough (Section 2.1.3): every rate constant appears once in the denominator. ✔
  3. Simulate dynamics (15 min): using a pre-made Colab notebook, students plot protein concentration over time and observe how long it takes to reach steady state.
  4. Explore parameter sensitivity (10 min): students change one parameter at a time (e.g., double the promoter strength, halve the degradation rate) and observe the effect on steady-state concentration and response time.

Discussion questions:

  1. If you wanted to double the GFP output, which parameter change would be most effective? Why?
  2. What happens if the protein degradation rate is very low? Is there a downside to extremely stable proteins?
  3. How does plasmid copy number affect the result? What are the biological trade-offs of high-copy plasmids?
  4. If the promoter were inducible (turned on by a chemical), how would you modify the equation?

2.2.1 The AI Paradigm Shift: Function → Structure → Sequence

What this demonstrates: the inversion of the Central Dogma as a design principle — the clearest illustration of Module 1’s claim that synbio asks “what could this system do?” rather than “how does this system work?”

For decades, biology followed a one-directional flow: DNA → RNA → protein sequence → 3D structure → function. This is the Central Dogma. Understanding a protein meant studying it from the gene up.

AI protein design inverts this flow. Instead of starting with a gene and asking “what does this protein do?”, we start with a desired function and ask “what protein would do this?” The new flow is:

Desired Function (e.g., "bind to disease protein X")
        │
        ▼
Target Structure (the 3D shape needed to perform the function)
        │
        ▼
Protein Sequence (the amino acid sequence that folds into that shape)
        │
        ▼
DNA Sequence (the gene that encodes the protein — for wet-lab synthesis)

Teacher analogy

Traditional biology is like reverse-engineering a machine — you take it apart to understand how it works. AI protein design is like engineering a machine — you decide what you want it to do, design the parts, and build it from scratch.

This shift was made possible by three breakthroughs:

  1. AlphaFold 2 (2020): DeepMind’s AI could predict protein 3D structure from amino acid sequence with near-experimental accuracy. This solved the 50-year-old “protein folding problem” and proved that AI could reason about protein physics. (Recognised with the 2024 Nobel Prize in Chemistry — see Module 1, Section 1.3.)
  2. RFdiffusion (2023) → RFdiffusion3 (2025): researchers at the University of Washington Institute for Protein Design and RosettaCommons adapted the structure-prediction architecture to run in reverse — instead of predicting structure from sequence, it generates new structures from functional specifications. The third generation, RFdiffusion3 (released open-source in December 2025), diffuses at the atomic level and can design proteins that interact with proteins, DNA, and small molecules. This is like having an AI that can not only recognise faces but also generate new ones.
  3. ProteinMPNN (2022): a neural network that solves the “inverse folding problem” — given a 3D protein backbone, it predicts the optimal amino acid sequence that will fold into that shape. This is the sequence-design counterpart to RFdiffusion’s backbone generation. (Its extension, LigandMPNN (2024) — used in the pipeline below — additionally accounts for bound ligands and target context when designing sequences.)

2.2.2 What Is De Novo Protein Design?

De novo (Latin: “from the beginning”) protein design is the process of creating entirely new protein sequences that do not exist in nature, designed to perform a specific function. The most common application is designing protein binders — small proteins or peptides that stick to a specific target molecule (like a disease-related protein) with high specificity.

The general workflow is:

  1. Choose a target (e.g., a disease protein with a known 3D structure)
  2. Generate a protein “backbone” — the 3D scaffold shape — that fits against the target
  3. Assign amino acid sequences to that backbone
  4. Validate: does the designed protein actually fold into the intended shape?
  5. Score: does it bind the target strongly? Specifically? Safely?
  6. Iterate: improve the design through rounds of mutation and re-evaluation

Teacher analogy

Imagine you want to design a key that fits a specific lock (the target protein). Step 2 is designing the shape of the key (the backbone). Step 3 is choosing what material each part of the key is made from (the amino acid sequence). Steps 4–6 are testing whether the key actually fits the lock, and refining it until it does.

The Design Funnel

The pipeline implements a funnel — a multi-stage filtering process that starts with many candidate designs and progressively narrows them down:

target PDB + hotspots
        │
        ▼
 1. DESIGN    → Generate protein backbones using RFdiffusion3 (AI generates 3D shapes)
 2. SEQUENCE  → Assign amino acids to backbones using LigandMPNN (AI fills in the sequence)
 3. VALIDATE  → Check if the sequence folds into the intended shape using RosettaFold-3 (AI self-check)
 4. iPTM      → Co-fold binder + target together using Boltz-2 (AI predicts the complex)
 5. INTERFACE → Calculate binding strength using PRODIGY (physics-based scoring)
 6. MHC       → Check if the binder might trigger an immune response (immunogenicity screening)
 7. OFF-TARGET→ Check if the binder accidentally binds other proteins (selectivity screening)
 8. SCORE     → Combine all metrics into a single ranking with tiers
 9. EVOLVE    → Mutate the best designs and re-test (in silico directed evolution)
10. REPORT    → Generate a ranked list of the best candidates

Teacher analogy

Think of this as a talent show with multiple rounds. Round 1 (design) generates many contestants. Each subsequent round eliminates those who fail a specific test — can they fold correctly? Do they bind the target? Are they safe? By the final round, only the best candidates remain.

2.2.4 In Silico Directed Evolution [Deep dive]

One of the most powerful features of the pipeline is in silico directed evolution — a computational analogue of natural selection applied to protein design.

The process:

  1. Start with the top-ranked candidates from the design funnel (parents)
  2. Apply random mutations to each parent (children):
    • 1–3 amino acid substitutions per child
    • Constraints: preserve cysteines; avoid oxidation-prone residues (M, W, free C); avoid chemical-liability motifs prone to deamidation or isomerisation (e.g., NG, DG)
  3. Re-run the children through the funnel (validate → co-fold → score)
  4. Select the best-performing children as parents for the next generation
  5. Repeat for N generations

Parent selection uses an Upper Confidence Bound (UCB) algorithm, which balances:

  • Exploitation: choosing parents with the highest known scores
  • Exploration: giving chances to less-tested but potentially promising candidates

Curriculum connection

This directly mirrors natural selection (variation → selection → inheritance) and can be used to teach evolutionary concepts (HKDSE Compulsory Part II, IB Theme D, IAL Topic 4). The key difference: computational evolution runs in minutes, while natural evolution takes millions of years.

2.3 Molecular Dynamics (MD) Simulations

Scope note for teachers

2.3.1 Overview and Logic Flow

What this demonstrates: the difference between a static structure and a dynamic molecule — a concept students meet implicitly whenever the syllabus mentions protein shape and enzyme flexibility.

Molecular dynamics (MD) simulation is a computational method that simulates the physical movements of atoms and molecules over time. While AI structure prediction (Section 2.2) gives us a “photograph” of a protein — a single static structure — MD gives us a “video” showing how the protein and its surroundings move, flex, and interact over time.

The core principle: every atom in a molecular system experiences forces from every other atom — electrostatic attractions and repulsions, van der Waals interactions, bond stretching, angle bending, and torsional rotations. MD calculates these forces using a force field (a set of physics equations and parameters) and then uses Newton’s laws of motion to update the positions of all atoms at each time step.

The workflow:

  1. INITIAL STRUCTURE — start with a 3D structure (from AI prediction, X-ray crystallography, or NMR)
  2. SOLVATION — place the protein in a box of water molecules (simulate physiological conditions)
  3. ENERGY MINIMISATION — relax the system to remove steric clashes (atoms too close together)
  4. EQUILIBRATION — gradually heat the system to the target temperature (e.g., 310 K = 37 °C)
  5. PRODUCTION RUN — simulate for a set time (e.g., 10–100 nanoseconds), recording positions every 1–2 picoseconds
  6. ANALYSIS — calculate RMSD, RMSF, radius of gyration, hydrogen bonds, binding energy, etc.

Teacher analogy

“AI structure prediction is like taking a photograph of a car — you see its shape but not how it drives. MD simulation is like putting the car on a test track and filming it — you see how the suspension flexes, how the tyres grip, and whether the engine overheats. Both are useful, but they answer different questions.”

2.4 Virtual Cloning & DNA Sequence Assembly

2.4.1 Why Virtual Cloning?

What this demonstrates: the planning discipline of the BUILD stage — and the cheapest place to make (and fix) mistakes.

Before students touch a pipette, they can plan and verify their entire cloning strategy on a computer. Virtual cloning:

  • Prevents costly mistakes: catching a wrong restriction site in silico saves reagents, time, and frustration.
  • Builds conceptual understanding: seeing plasmid maps and assembly strategies visually reinforces how DNA parts fit together.
  • Enables complex designs: multi-part assemblies (e.g., promoter + RBS + gene + terminator) can be planned and verified before ordering any DNA.

Teacher analogy

Virtual cloning is like doing a dry run of a recipe before cooking. You lay out all the ingredients, check that you have the right tools, and walk through each step on paper. When you actually cook, everything goes smoothly because you’ve already anticipated the problems.

2.4.3 In Silico Assembly Strategies

Three assembly methods are commonly used in synthetic biology. Each can be simulated virtually before executing at the bench:

BioBrick Assembly (Standard Assembly)

Principle: uses a standardised set of restriction enzyme sites (EcoRI, XbaI, SpeI, PstI) flanking each DNA part. Parts can be assembled in a standardised “cut-and-paste” manner.

Part A (in plasmid):  [EcoRI] --- Part A --- [XbaI]   [SpeI] --- [PstI]
Part B (in plasmid):  [EcoRI] --- [XbaI] --- Part B   [SpeI] --- [PstI]
Assembly:
  Cut Part A plasmid with EcoRI + SpeI  →  [EcoRI] --- Part A --- [SpeI]
  Cut Part B plasmid with XbaI + PstI   →  [XbaI] --- Part B --- [PstI]
  Ligate  →  [EcoRI] --- Part A --- [XbaI/SpeI scar] --- Part B

Note

The XbaI and SpeI sites produce compatible overhangs. When ligated, they form a “scar” sequence that cannot be cut by either enzyme again. This is a feature (prevents re-cutting) and a limitation (the scar adds 8 bp between parts, which can affect spacing).

Virtual simulation in SnapGene:

  1. Open both plasmid maps
  2. Identify the EcoRI, XbaI, SpeI, and PstI sites
  3. Predict the fragment sizes after digestion
  4. Draw the expected gel electrophoresis pattern (connects to Module 3, Section 3.2.3)

Gibson Assembly

Principle: uses overlapping sequences (20–40 bp) at the ends of DNA fragments. A master mix containing exonuclease, polymerase, and ligase joins the fragments in a single-tube reaction at 50 °C.

Fragment 1:  5' --- [gene A] --- [overlap with B] 3'
Fragment 2:  5'  [overlap with A] --- [gene B] --- [overlap with C] 3'
Fragment 3:  5'  [overlap with B] --- [gene C] --- 3'
        + Gibson Master Mix +
Result:  [gene A] --- [gene B] --- [gene C]   (seamless, no scar)

Advantages over BioBrick:

  • No scar sequence between parts
  • Can assemble 2–6 fragments in one reaction
  • No restriction enzymes needed (cheaper, faster)

Virtual simulation:

  1. Design primers that add overlap regions to each fragment
  2. Check that the overlaps are unique (no cross-reactivity)
  3. Verify the final assembled sequence reads in-frame (if coding sequences are joined)

Restriction-Ligation Cloning

Principle: the classic cloning method — cut both the vector and insert with the same restriction enzyme(s), then ligate.

Vector:  [EcoRI] --- [BamHI] --- [rest of plasmid]
Insert:  [EcoRI] --- [your gene] --- [BamHI]
        + Cut both with EcoRI + BamHI + Ligate +
Result:  [EcoRI] --- [your gene] --- [BamHI] --- [rest of plasmid]

Virtual simulation in SnapGene:

  1. Open the vector plasmid
  2. Find unique restriction sites flanking the insertion point
  3. Verify the insert has matching sites
  4. Check for internal sites within the insert (would cause unwanted cutting)
  5. Predict the size of the resulting plasmid and confirm by simulated gel

2.4.4 Classroom Exercise: Planning a Cloning Strategy

Objective: students plan a restriction-ligation cloning strategy to modify the pack’s running-example plasmid, entirely in silico.

Setup (computer lab, 45 minutes):

  1. Download tools (5 min): install SnapGene Viewer and download the pSB1C3–BBa_K608010 plasmid map from the iGEM Registry (the GFP reporter cassette used throughout this pack).
  2. Analyse the vector (10 min):
    • Open pSB1C3–BBa_K608010 in SnapGene Viewer
    • Find the GFP coding sequence and its flanking restriction sites
    • Identify which restriction enzymes cut uniquely outside the GFP cassette
  3. Analyse the insert (10 min):
    • Choose a replacement gene (e.g., a red fluorescent protein from the iGEM Registry)
    • Check for internal restriction sites that match the vector’s enzymes
    • Identify enzymes that do NOT cut within the new gene
  4. Design the strategy (10 min):
    • Choose two enzymes (e.g., EcoRI and SpeI, the standard BioBrick flanks) that cut uniquely in the vector and do not cut within the insert
    • Determine the fragment sizes after digestion
    • Draw the expected gel pattern
  5. Verify (10 min):
    • Confirm the insert will be in the correct orientation (directional cloning with two different enzymes)
    • Check that the reading frame is maintained (if the gene needs to be expressed)
    • Predict the size of the recombinant plasmid

(Extension for advanced students: repeat the exercise with pUC19 — an ampicillin-resistant classical cloning vector with a multiple cloning site — and compare the two workflows.)

Discussion questions:

  1. Why use two different restriction enzymes instead of one? What would happen with a single enzyme?
  2. What would happen if one of your chosen enzymes also cut within the replacement gene?
  3. How would you verify that the cloning worked after transformation? (Connect to Module 3: gel electrophoresis and colony PCR — Section 3.2.4)
  4. If you wanted to express the new gene from this plasmid, what additional elements does the cassette already provide? (Promoter, RBS, terminator — identify them on the map.)

Common student difficulties:

Difficulty Cause Fix
Choosing enzymes that cut inside the insert Only checking the vector Make “check the insert first” an explicit step
Confusing the MCS with the whole plasmid Map-reading inexperience Colour-code features before starting; identify ori and resistance gene first
Ignoring orientation Single-enzyme habits Always ask: “which way does the gene face relative to the promoter?”

2.7 Assessment Ideas (SBA / IA Compatible)

The dry lab’s advantage for assessment: every exercise produces a digital artefact (plot, scorecard, cloning plan) that can be collected, compared, and moderated without a lab.

  1. Prediction-vs-simulation report (2.1.6): students submit their parameter-sensitivity results with a written explanation of why each parameter has the effect it does, referencing the steady-state equation. Assesses quantitative reasoning, not recall.

  3. Cloning plan write-up (2.4.4): students submit their enzyme-choice reasoning, predicted fragment sizes, and expected gel drawing. Module 3’s Session 4 then assesses the comparison between this prediction and the observed gel — a complete design–build–test–learn assessment arc spanning both modules.
  4. Error-diagnosis task (2.1/2.3): give students a deliberately flawed model output (e.g., a steady-state calculation with a unit error, or an RMSD plot showing unfolding) and ask them to diagnose the problem. Mirrors the diagnostic-writing assessment in Module 3, Section 3.4.4.

3.1.3 Sterile Technique in Non-Traditional Settings

Most secondary school laboratories do not have a laminar flow hood (biosafety cabinet). This is not an obstacle — experienced researchers routinely maintain adequate sterility using a Bunsen burner and good habits.

Working Without a Laminar Flow Hood

  • The Bunsen burner creates an updraft of hot air: work within 15–20 cm of a lit flame. The rising column of warm air prevents airborne particles from settling onto your work.
  • Reduce air movement: close windows, turn off air-conditioning vents near the bench, minimise walking past the workspace during critical steps.
  • Minimise exposure time: never leave tubes, plates, or media uncapped longer than necessary. Work with purpose and efficiency.
  • Spray the bench: wipe down with 70% ethanol before starting. Allow to dry completely before lighting the burner.
  • Face away from open containers: avoid breathing, coughing, or sneezing over open tubes, plates, or media.

Bunsen Burner Best Practices

  • Position the burner at one side of the workspace — not in the centre.
  • Use a roaring blue flame (fully open air hole) for the sterile zone; a quieter flame for heating.
  • Flame the mouth of glass bottles and conical flasks briefly when opening and closing.
  • Never leave the flame unattended.
  • Keep flammable solvents (ethanol, methanol) at least 30 cm from the burner.
  • Have a damp cloth or small fire blanket accessible.

SAFETY: Long hair must be tied back near a Bunsen burner. Loose sleeves must be rolled up or fastened. Ethanol spray bottles must NEVER be used near a lit flame.

3.1.4 Pipetting Mastery

The micropipette is the most used — and most often misused — instrument in any molecular biology laboratory. Pipetting errors cause inaccurate volumes, contaminated samples, and wasted reagents. Mastering pipetting before attempting any protocol dramatically improves outcomes.

Micropipette Types and Volume Ranges

Pipette Volume range Typical use Tip size
P2 0.2–2 µL Enzyme additions, concentrated DNA 10 µL
P10 1–10 µL Primers, enzymes, small DNA volumes 10 µL
P20 2–20 µL PCR components, restriction digests 10 µL
P200 20–200 µL PCR reagents, loading dye, buffer additions 200 µL
P1000 200–1000 µL Media, buffer volumes, overnight cultures 1000 µL

NOTE: Always set the volume before picking up the tip. Never rotate the volume dial past the maximum or minimum — this damages the piston mechanism.

Correct Pipetting Technique — Step by Step

  1. Set the volume: dial to the desired volume.
  2. Attach the tip: press the shaft firmly and straight down into a tip. A loose tip causes air leakage and volume errors.
  3. Aspirate: hold the pipette vertically. Depress the plunger to the first stop. Immerse the tip 2–3 mm into the liquid (not deeper). Release the plunger slowly and smoothly — rapid release causes bubbles and inaccuracy.
  4. Dispense: touch the tip against the wall of the receiving tube. Depress to the first stop, pause 1 second, then press to the second stop to expel all remaining liquid.
  5. Eject the tip: press the tip-ejector button. Never touch tips with bare fingers.

Avoiding Aerosol Contamination

Aerosol contamination — microscopic droplets transferring between samples — is the leading cause of false positives in PCR and cross-contamination in cloning. It is invisible and almost impossible to detect after the fact.

  • Use filter (barrier) tips: an internal hydrophobic filter prevents liquid from entering the pipette barrel even if you over-aspirate. Mandatory for PCR setup and any work with DNA templates. Buy them sterile-wrapped and DNase-free, or autoclave loose tips before use — non-sterile tips are a common source of failed first sessions.
  • Never reuse tips: change tips between every sample, every tube, every reagent. Even a brief touch of a tip to a different surface can transfer nanograms of DNA — enough to contaminate a PCR.
  • Work from clean to dirty: add water and buffer first, template DNA last. If a tip accidentally touches the template, discard it — do not use it to dispense into the master mix.
  • Dedicated pipettes for DNA work: designate specific pipettes for pre-PCR (clean reagents) and post-PCR (products, gels) work. PCR products are extraordinarily concentrated and will contaminate pre-PCR reagents permanently.
  • Negative control: always include a no-template control (NTC) — a PCR tube with water instead of DNA template. If the NTC produces a band, aerosol contamination has occurred.

Handling Viscous Liquids

Glycerol stocks and enzyme stocks do not flow like water; standard technique causes significant volume errors.

3.1.5 Culture Handling Basics

Three routine skills underpin every protocol in Section 3.2 — and are themselves excellent, low-cost student practicals.

Streaking for Single Colonies

Purpose: isolate individual clones from a mixed population. Every downstream experiment (transformation readout, colony PCR, overnight culture) depends on working from a single colony.

  1. Flame an inoculating loop (or use a sterile disposable loop) and let it cool — touch it to a sterile part of the agar to test (it should not sizzle or melt agar).
  2. Touch a colony or the stock, and streak a small patch in Zone 1 of a fresh LB agar plate.
  3. Flame/replace the loop, rotate the plate 90°, drag through Zone 1 once, and streak into Zone 2.
  1. Repeat for Zones 3 and 4, diluting the cells each time.
  2. Incubate inverted at 37 °C overnight. Single, well-separated colonies should appear in Zones 3–4.

Expected result: distinct, isolated colonies in the later zones. A “lawn” in all zones means the loop was not cooled or not diluted between zones.

Overnight Culture

Purpose: grow a liquid suspension of a single clone for plasmid DNA, glycerol stocks, or experiments.

  1. Add 3–5 mL LB + appropriate antibiotic to a sterile 15 mL tube.
  2. Pick a single colony with a sterile tip or loop and swirl it in the tube.
  3. Incubate at 37 °C with shaking (~200 rpm) for 12–16 h. Without shaking, growth is slower — allow up to 24 h in a static incubator.

Expected result: a visibly turbid (cloudy) suspension the next day.

Glycerol Stocks for Long-Term Storage

Purpose: preserve a clone without continuous re-streaking.

  1. Mix 500 µL overnight culture + 500 µL sterile 50% glycerol in a cryotube (final glycerol ~25%).
  2. Freeze at −80 °C (years) or −20 °C (weeks to months; viability declines).
  3. To recover: scrape a small amount from the frozen stock with a sterile loop and streak onto fresh agar. Never thaw an entire stock repeatedly.

School without a −80 °C freezer?

Maintain the strain by re-streaking a fresh plate every 1–2 weeks from the current working plate, and request an iBRIDGE loaner slot or a HKU lab day for long-term archiving.

3.2.1 Heat-Shock Transformation of E. coli

What This Demonstrates

Transformation is the process by which bacteria take up foreign DNA — here, an engineered plasmid — from their environment. It is the central step that converts a designed genetic circuit into a living biological system, and the moment the dry lab’s design becomes physical reality.

Curriculum anchor

HKDSE Compulsory Part II (molecular genetics) and Elective Biotechnology; IB Theme D; IAL Topic 2. Selection on antibiotic plates demonstrates selection — a concept students meet again in evolution.

Key concepts:

  • Plasmid: a small circular DNA molecule (2–10 kb) that replicates independently of the bacterial chromosome. Carries the gene of interest, an antibiotic resistance marker, and an origin of replication (ori).
  • Competent cells: E. coli treated with CaCl₂ to make membranes temporarily permeable to DNA. Commercial preparations (e.g., NEB 5-alpha, a DH5α-format strain) offer the highest efficiency and are strongly recommended for schools.
  • Heat shock: a brief, precise 30–45 s exposure to 42 °C that drives DNA into the cell. Timing is critical.
  • Outgrowth recovery: post-heat-shock incubation in SOC or LB broth at 37 °C for 45–60 min, allowing bacteria to express the antibiotic resistance gene before encountering selective media.
  • Selective plating: only cells that took up the plasmid and express the resistance gene survive on antibiotic-containing agar.
  • Positive control: transformation with a known-working plasmid — confirms the competent cells are functional.
  • Negative control: transformation with water (no DNA) — should yield zero colonies on selective media.

Materials

  • Chemically competent E. coli DH5α-format cells (store at −80 °C)
  • Plasmid DNA (1–10 ng/µL in dH₂O or TE buffer)
  • SOC or LB broth (room temperature, no antibiotic)
  • Sterile 1.5 mL microcentrifuge tubes
  • Ice bucket + crushed ice
  • 42 °C water bath or heat block (±0.5 °C accuracy is essential)
  • 37 °C incubator (for recovery and overnight growth)
  • LB agar plates + chloramphenicol (for pSB1C3; match the antibiotic to your plasmid’s marker), pre-warmed to 37 °C
  • Micropipettes (P10, P200) + sterile filtered tips
  • Sterile cell spreader, timer, gloves, lab coat

Protocol

  1. Thaw on ice — remove the competent cell tube from −80 °C and thaw on ice for 5–10 min. Never thaw at room temperature. Keep on ice at all times.
  2. Add DNA — add 1–5 µL plasmid DNA to the competent cells. Mix by flicking the tube gently 4–5 times. Do not vortex.
  3. Incubate on ice for 20–30 min. DNA associates with the cell surface.
  4. Heat shock — transfer to a pre-warmed 42 °C water bath for exactly 30 s (DH5α) or 45 s (TOP10). Use a timer. This is the most critical step.
  5. Return to ice immediately for 2 min.
  6. Add recovery medium — add 250–950 µL room-temperature SOC or LB broth. No antibiotic.
  7. Recover — incubate at 37 °C for 45–60 min with gentle agitation (~200 rpm) if available.
  8. Plate — spread 100–200 µL onto pre-warmed selective plates. Incubate inverted at 37 °C overnight (12–16 h).
  9. Count and record colonies the next morning.

Expected Result

Discrete, well-separated colonies on the selective plate (each a clone of one successfully transformed bacterium); no growth on the negative-control plate; abundant colonies on the positive-control plate. With the GFP plasmid, colonies glow green under blue light — the single most motivating photograph in a school synbio programme.

Quantify your result: transformation efficiency = colonies ÷ (µg DNA transformed × fraction of the culture plated). Have students count colonies, compute the efficiency, and compare across classes or conditions — genuine data handling with a defensible uncertainty discussion (used as an SBA/IA assessment in Section 3.4.4).

SAFETY: E. coli K-12 is BSL-1. Wear gloves and lab coat. Decontaminate all bacterial waste with 10% bleach (30 min) or autoclave before disposal. Wash hands after leaving the lab.

3.2.2 Polymerase Chain Reaction (PCR)

What This Demonstrates

PCR amplifies a specific DNA sequence exponentially — from a single copy to billions — in 2–3 hours. In synbio it is used to amplify genes for cloning, verify successful insertions (colony PCR, Section 3.2.4), and introduce specific mutations.

Curriculum anchor

HKDSE Compulsory Parts I–II (DNA replication, enzymes, molecular genetics); IB Themes A/D; IAL Topic 2. The three-step cycle is DNA replication taught as a machine: denaturation, annealing, extension.

The three-step cycle: (1) Denaturation at 95 °C — strands separate; (2) Annealing at 50–65 °C — primers bind their target sequences; (3) Extension at 72 °C — Taq polymerase builds new strands. Repeat 30× → exponential amplification.

Key concepts:

  • Primers: short synthetic DNA oligonucleotides (18–25 nt) that define and flank the region to be amplified. The forward primer binds the antisense strand; the reverse primer binds the sense strand.
  • Tm (melting temperature): the temperature at which half of the primer–template duplexes are dissociated. Annealing temperature is typically set ~5 °C below the lowest primer Tm. Use the NEB Tm Calculator or similar tool.
  • Taq polymerase: thermostable DNA polymerase from Thermus aquaticus. Extends at ~1 kb/min at 72 °C. Extension time = amplicon size (kb) × 1 min.
  • Master mix: a premixed combination of all PCR reagents except template and primers — reduces pipetting steps and errors when running multiple samples.
  • Amplicon: the PCR product — the specific DNA fragment defined by the primer pair.
  • No-template control (NTC): a tube with water instead of DNA template. Any band here = contamination.

3.2.3 Agarose Gel Electrophoresis

What This Demonstrates

Gel electrophoresis separates DNA fragments by size using an electric field. It is the primary quality-control step after PCR and restriction digests, providing direct visual evidence of whether an experiment worked.

Curriculum anchor

HKDSE Elective Biotechnology; SBA data analysis (band-size estimation is a genuine quantitative exercise — see Section 3.4.4); IB Theme A/IA; IAL A2 practical skills.

The principle: DNA is negatively charged and migrates toward the positive electrode (red terminal — “run to red”). Smaller fragments travel faster through the gel matrix; larger fragments move more slowly. After staining, bands are visible under blue LED or UV light.

Why can we see the bands? DNA itself is invisible. Two reagents make it visible:

  • Loading dye (the 6× dye mixed into each sample) does two jobs: its glycerol makes the sample denser than the buffer so it sinks into the well instead of floating away, and its coloured dyes (bromophenol blue, xylene cyanol) migrate ahead of the DNA so you can watch the run’s progress. The coloured dye front is not the DNA.
  • The stain (GelRed or SYBR Safe, added to the gel) binds to the DNA and fluoresces under blue LED light — that fluorescence is what you photograph as bands.

Gel Percentage Guide

Agarose % Fragment size range Best used for
0.7% 5–50 kb Large DNA fragments (genomic, BAC inserts)
1.0% 500 bp–10 kb Standard PCR products, most restriction digests
1.5% 200 bp–3 kb Smaller PCR products, multiplex PCR
2.0% 50 bp–2 kb Short amplicons, miRNA, oligonucleotides

Materials

  • Agarose powder (molecular biology grade)
  • 1× TAE buffer (for gel and tank — same buffer in both)
  • Safe DNA stain: GelRed or SYBR Safe (never ethidium bromide in school settings)
  • DNA ladder (100 bp or 1 kb Plus — choose based on expected fragment sizes)
  • 6× DNA loading dye (glycerol for density + bromophenol blue tracking dye)
  • Gel electrophoresis tank + power supply, casting tray, comb
  • Microwave (for melting agarose) + heat-resistant gloves
  • Blue LED transilluminator + camera (preferred over UV for safety)

Protocol

  1. Cast the gel: dissolve agarose in 1× TAE in a conical flask (e.g., 0.5 g in 50 mL for a 1% gel). Microwave in 30 s bursts, swirling between each, until fully clear. Cool to ~60 °C. Add GelRed (5 µL per 50 mL). Pour into the casting tray with comb. Set for 20–30 min.
  2. Set up the tank: remove the comb. Place the gel in the tank with wells nearest the negative electrode (black). Fill with 1× TAE until the gel is submerged by 2–3 mm.
  1. Prepare samples: mix 5 µL PCR product + 1 µL 6× loading dye. Keep the DNA ladder unmixed (most commercial ladders already contain loading dye — check the instructions).
  2. Load samples: using gel-loading tips, carefully load each sample into the bottom of a well. Always load the DNA ladder in lane 1.
  3. Run: connect the power supply. Apply 80–120 V (lower voltage = sharper bands but slower run). Run until the dye front is ~2/3 of the way down the gel (~30–45 min).
  4. Visualise: switch off the power. Transfer the gel to a blue LED transilluminator. Photograph.

SAFETY: NEVER use ethidium bromide in school settings — it is a potent mutagen. GelRed and SYBR Safe are safe alternatives compatible with blue LED systems. UV light damages eyes and skin — always use proper eye protection if UV is unavoidable.

PRACTICAL TIP: Running gels at 80 V rather than 120 V gives sharper, cleaner bands and greatly reduces the risk of overheating or gel melting — important for thin classroom gels.

Expected Result

One or more discrete, bright, sharp bands at the expected size(s), visible against a dark background under blue LED illumination; sharp, evenly spaced ladder bands; straight dye front.

Quantify your result: estimate band sizes by plotting ladder band migration distance against log(size), fitting a straight line, and interpolating the unknown band’s size — a classic semi-log analysis, directly SBA/IA-compatible (Section 3.4.4).

3.2.4 Verifying Clones: Colony PCR and Diagnostic Digest

What This Demonstrates

The verification step of the engineering cycle. After transformation, “colonies grew” does not mean “the construct is correct” — colonies might contain the empty vector, a re-ligated vector, or a wrong insert. Verification closes the loop between the cloning strategy planned in Module 2, Section 2.4 and the construct actually built.

Curriculum anchor

HKDSE Elective Biotechnology; SBA/IB IA — verification is the “evaluate the method” step that examiners reward.

Option A: Colony PCR (recommended for schools — fast, cheap, no DNA purification)

  1. Label PCR tubes for several colonies (e.g., 6–8 candidates) plus an NTC.
  2. Add 20 µL sterile water to each of an equivalent set of small tubes.
  3. Pick each colony with a sterile pipette tip; swirl the tip in its water tube, then also patch the colony onto a “master plate” (labelled grid agar) so the clone is not lost.
  4. Use 1 µL of each colony–water suspension directly as template in the standard 25 µL reaction (Section 3.2.2), with primers flanking the insertion site.
  5. Modify the program: extend the initial denaturation to 5 min at 95 °C to lyse the cells and release DNA.
  6. Run on a gel (Section 3.2.3).

Interpreting the result: primers flanking the insertion site give a small band for the empty vector and a larger band for the correct insert (band size difference = insert size). Primers designed with one primer inside the insert give a band only when the insert is present. Either design works — decide it in the dry lab first.

Option B: Diagnostic Restriction Digest (requires purified plasmid)

  1. Isolate plasmid DNA with a commercial miniprep kit (teacher demonstration or advanced students).
  2. Digest 0.5–1 µg plasmid with the two enzymes flanking the insertion site (chosen in Module 2, Section 2.4), in the supplier’s buffer, 37 °C for 1 h.
  3. Run on a 1% gel alongside the undigested plasmid.

Interpreting the result: the digest should yield two bands — the vector backbone and the insert — at the sizes predicted in the dry lab. An undigested or singly-cut pattern indicates the construct is wrong.

Which to choose?

Colony PCR for classroom teaching (no miniprep kits needed); diagnostic digest for iGEM-team preparation (it is the professional standard and reinforces the restriction logic learned in Module 2).

3.2.5 Optional Advanced: SDS-PAGE and Western Blot Basics

When to use this section: for advanced clubs, iGEM-team preparation, or demonstrating protein-level expression where equipment already exists. Not recommended as a core classroom practical (see Section 3.0.1).

While PCR/gel electrophoresis confirms DNA-level success, SDS-PAGE and Western blotting confirm that the gene has been expressed as a protein. SDS-PAGE separates all proteins by size; Western blotting uses antibodies to identify one specific protein in a complex mixture.

Expected result (SDS-PAGE): a pattern of blue bands (Coomassie-stained) across the gel; the protein of interest appears as a band at the expected molecular weight (kDa), often more intense than background if well overexpressed.

Expected result (Western blot): a single band on the membrane at the exact molecular weight of the target protein — no other bands if the antibody is specific and blocking was effective.

SDS-PAGE — Key Concepts

  • SDS (sodium dodecyl sulphate): a detergent that denatures proteins and coats them with uniform negative charge proportional to mass, enabling size-based separation.
  • Polyacrylamide gel: a synthetic matrix (higher % = smaller pores = better resolution of small proteins). Only pre-cast gels should be used in schools — acrylamide monomer is neurotoxic.
  • Sample denaturation: proteins are mixed with SDS loading buffer with reducing agent (DTT or β-mercaptoethanol) and heated to 95 °C for 5 min to fully unfold before loading.
  • Coomassie Brilliant Blue: non-specific protein stain; sensitivity ~0.5–1 µg per band.
  • Protein ladder: mixture of known-MW proteins run alongside samples as a size reference.

SDS-PAGE — Simplified Protocol

  1. Sample prep: mix protein sample with 4× SDS loading buffer (+ DTT). Heat at 95 °C for 5 min. Spin briefly.
  2. Gel setup: use a pre-cast gel (e.g., Bio-Rad Mini-PROTEAN). Assemble in the electrophoresis unit with 1× SDS running buffer.
  3. Load: denatured samples (10–20 µL) and protein ladder into wells.
  4. Run: 100–200 V until the dye front reaches the gel base (~45–90 min).
  5. Stain: submerge in Coomassie staining solution (1 h). Destain until bands are clear.
  6. Photograph the gel.

Western Blot — Key Concepts

  • Transfer: proteins are electrophoretically moved from the gel onto a PVDF or nitrocellulose membrane, making them accessible to antibodies.
  • Blocking: the membrane is soaked in 5% non-fat milk in TBST to prevent non-specific antibody binding.
  • Primary antibody: binds specifically to the target protein; incubated overnight at 4 °C.
  • Secondary antibody: binds the primary antibody; conjugated to HRP enzyme for signal generation.
  • ECL detection: HRP substrate (enhanced chemiluminescence) produces light at target-protein locations, captured by camera or film.
  • Loading control: a protein at constant level (e.g., β-actin) confirming equal loading across lanes.

Aliquoting strategy

Pre-mixing a class master mix (all shared PCR reagents except template and primers) reduces each student’s pipetting from 7 steps to 2–3 — fewer errors, faster sessions, and it converts the biggest source of class failure into a teacher-controlled step. Students still perform the template and primer additions themselves.

3.4.3 Class Management Notes

  • Team roles within each pair/trio: pipettor, timer/notebook, materials — rotate every session so everyone pipettes.
  • One “sacrificial” demonstration reaction run by the teacher alongside student reactions gives the class a known-good reference if all student reactions fail.
  • Photograph everything. Plate photos and gel photos are the raw material for assessment and for the students’ portfolios.
  • Failure is data. A failed transformation diagnosed with Section 3.3 is a better lesson than an unexamined success — build the diagnosis into the session, not around it.

3.4.4 Assessment Ideas (SBA / IA Compatible)

  1. Transformation efficiency (quantitative): efficiency = colonies ÷ (µg DNA transformed × fraction of the culture plated). Students compute and compare across classes/conditions — genuine data handling with a defensible uncertainty discussion.
  2. Band-size estimation (graphical): plot ladder band migration distance vs. log(size), fit a line, interpolate the unknown band size, and compare to the predicted size. This is a classic semi-log analysis, directly SBA/IA-compatible.
  3. Prediction vs. outcome (scientific reasoning): students compare their Session 1 colony-count predictions and Session 3 predicted gels against observations, and must explain any discrepancy using Section 3.3 — assessment of scientific method, not recall.
  4. Diagnostic writing task: give students an anonymised “failed” gel photo; they write a diagnosis and a corrected protocol. Tests Section 3.3 mastery directly.

A.1 Biological Safety

  • Organism: E. coli K-12 derivatives only (BSL-1 / Risk Group 1). These strains cannot colonise the human gut, lack virulence factors, and survive poorly outside laboratory conditions.
  • Personal protection: lab coat, nitrile gloves, eye protection, closed-toe shoes at all times. Tie back long hair; roll up loose sleeves near flames.
  • No mouth pipetting, no eating or drinking in the laboratory, hand-washing on exit.
  • Spills: cover with paper towels soaked in 10% bleach; leave 30 min; wipe up; dispose of towels as biological waste.

A.2 Regulatory Note for Hong Kong Schools

What the law covers — and what it does not

Hong Kong’s Genetically Modified Organisms (Control of Release) Ordinance (Cap. 607), in force since 1 March 2011, implements the Cartagena Protocol on Biosafety. It controls two things: the release of GMOs into the environment (which requires prior approval from the Director of Agriculture, Fisheries and Conservation) and the import/export of GMOs (which requires prescribed accompanying documents).

Under the Ordinance, a GMO is in “contained use” when it is handled within a facility or other physical barrier, with specific measures that effectively limit its contact with and impact on the environment. Classroom work in this pack — GM E. coli K-12 grown in sealed tubes and plates inside a school laboratory, with all waste chemically decontaminated or autoclaved before disposal — is contained use, not environmental release. AFCD’s own guidance lists “GM micro-organisms cultured in laboratories” as the canonical example of contained use, and prior approval is not required for contained use.

Two practical consequences for schools: 1. Never release. Plates, cultures, and contaminated consumables must be decontaminated (10% bleach, 30 min, or autoclave) before disposal — this is both good biosafety practice and what keeps the work squarely within contained use. The protocols in this module make decontamination a mandatory final step of every session. 2. Ordering materials. Commercially supplied plasmids and competent cells imported from outside Hong Kong are technically GMO shipments intended for contained use, which require prescribed documentation on import/export. In practice, established biological suppliers handle this documentation; order through standard suppliers or the iBRIDGE procurement channel rather than carrying biological materials across borders yourself.
